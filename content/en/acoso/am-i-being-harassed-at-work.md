You are suffering workplace harassment when, repeatedly and over a period of time, someone at work treats you with hostility or humiliates you, and this harms your dignity or your health. A one-off argument is not harassment, and neither is your boss requiring you to do your job. The key lies in the repetition, the degrading treatment and the harm it causes.

## What workplace harassment is

Workplace harassment, also called psychological harassment (acoso moral) or mobbing, is a form of psychological violence at work. There do not have to be blows or insults shouted at you. It is often more subtle: they isolate you, strip you of your duties, ridicule you or make your life impossible little by little.

Judges usually look at three things:

- **Hostile or humiliating conduct.** Acts that belittle you, isolate you or degrade you.
- **Repetition over time.** It is not a bad day, it is a pattern.
- **A real effect on you.** Harm to your dignity, your moral integrity or your health. Often, though not always, the conduct is aimed at making you leave.

Spanish law protects your dignity and your moral integrity at work. Convention 190 of the International Labour Organization (ILO) on violence and harassment has been in force in Spain since 25 May 2023 and strengthens that protection.

## What workplace harassment is not

Being able to tell the difference helps you avoid spending your energy on a weak claim and recognise a strong case in time.

### A one-off conflict

A heated argument with a coworker or a telling-off from your boss on a particular day is not usually harassment on its own. It may be disrespectful, and it may have consequences, but harassment requires a pattern.

### A legitimate demand

Being asked to keep to your working hours, meet reasonable targets or correct mistakes is part of the company's management authority (poder de dirección). In principle, an organisational change that affects all staff equally is not harassment either.

### A generally bad atmosphere

A stressful job or poor organisation may be a psychosocial risk that the company must prevent. But it is not the same as harassment directed at you.

> Be careful: the line is not always clear. A "legitimate" demand that is applied only to you, disproportionately and to humiliate you, can indeed form part of harassment.

## Signs that it may be harassment

These behaviours appear frequently in harassment cases. If you recognise several of them and they keep happening, it is a warning sign.

- They shout at you, insult you or mock you in front of others.
- They imitate you, ridicule the way you speak or your gestures.
- They leave you with no work, or give you pointless tasks or tasks far below your job category.
- They set you impossible deadlines so that you fail.
- They isolate you: they do not invite you to meetings, do not pass you information, forbid others from talking to you.
- They criticise everything you do, for no reason and constantly.
- They spread rumours about you or your private life.
- They change your post, your hours or your workplace to punish you.
- They threaten you with dismissal or with groundless disciplinary action.
- They watch your every move in a way they do not with others.

On top of this come the effects on your health: insomnia, anxiety before going to work, crying, digestive problems, feelings of guilt or that "you are doing something wrong". The fact that this is happening to you does not mean you are imagining it.

## Types of harassment depending on who does it

### Top-down harassment

Top-down harassment (acoso descendente) is carried out by a superior against someone who reports to them. It is the most common. For example, a boss who humiliates an employee to force them out. In 2005, Social Court No. 29 of Madrid (Juzgado de lo Social n.º 29 de Madrid) ordered a company and the harassing director, jointly and severally (de forma solidaria), to pay 30,000 euros to an employee whom she shouted at, imitated and humiliated to make him leave. It was a case handled by this firm.

### Horizontal harassment

Horizontal harassment (acoso horizontal) is carried out by coworkers at the same level. The company is not left out of it: if it knows and does not act, it is also liable. We explain it in [harassment by coworkers](/en/workplace-harassment/harassment-by-coworkers).

### Bottom-up harassment

Bottom-up harassment (acoso ascendente) is carried out by one or more subordinates against their superior. It is less frequent, but it exists: boycotts, organised disobedience, rumours to discredit the boss.

In addition to psychological harassment, the law specifically regulates sexual harassment and sex-based harassment (acoso sexual y acoso por razón de sexo). They have their own rules and their own protocol, which is compulsory in all companies.

## How long it has to last

There is no minimum period set by law. Some psychologists use as a reference behaviour that happens at least weekly for around six months, but the courts do not require that number. What they assess is whether there is a repeated pattern and harm.

A few very serious episodes in close succession may be enough. And conversely, many years of minor friction with no intention to cause harm may not be. It depends on each case.

> What matters is not waiting until you "have enough". Start writing things down and keeping evidence today, even if you do not yet know whether it is harassment.

## What to do if you think you are being harassed

1. **Write down what happens.** Date, time, what happened, who was there. We explain it in [how to prove harassment](/en/workplace-harassment/how-to-prove-workplace-harassment).
2. **Keep everything.** Emails, messages, changes of duties, work reports.
3. **Look after your health.** See your doctor and tell them what is happening at work. If you need sick leave, read [sick leave for anxiety due to harassment](/en/workplace-harassment/sick-leave-for-anxiety-due-to-harassment).
4. **Do not leave the company on your own initiative.** If you resign, you lose your compensation and your unemployment benefit (paro). There are other ways out, which we explain in [quitting your job because of harassment](/en/workplace-harassment/quitting-your-job-because-of-harassment).
5. **Get advice early.** Some deadlines are short and a wrong first step can weigh against you later.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a single very serious episode be harassment?

In general, harassment requires repetition. A very serious isolated episode may be a different offence, or even a crime, but it is not usually classed as workplace harassment.

### Do they have to intend to get rid of me?

Not always. Often that intention exists, but what is decisive is the repeated hostile treatment and the harm to your dignity or your health.

### What if my boss says they are only demanding performance?

Demanding performance is legitimate. Doing it with contempt, only with you, or with impossible targets so that you fail, is not. It is assessed as a whole.

### Do I need a psychologist's report to know whether it is harassment?

Not to get started. But if your health suffers, the medical or psychological report will be important evidence later on.
